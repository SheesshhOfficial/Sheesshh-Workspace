Ideas & Design

Five possible game experiences for Classes 1–5. Compare what the child does, how the world changes and how learning grows. No idea has been selected for implementation.

Confirmed goal: interactive, immersive games with good visuals for Classes 1–5. Open: concept, theme, subjects, syllabus, language, target device, orientation and first playable mission.

Design principles

  • Learning through action: the child sorts, builds, investigates or changes something, rather than only choosing answers on a quiz overlay.
  • Visible consequences: a useful action causes a clear world response.
  • Supportive experimentation: mistakes trigger a helpful consequence or hint, not humiliation or a payment/lives barrier.
  • Coherent visuals: original characters, objects and environments share one style and make the task readable.
  • Appropriate challenge: class-level variants change reasoning and scaffolding, not just speed or number size.
  • Bounded scope: prove one excellent mission before promising an entire world or curriculum.

Living Village

Game function

Help a small community through hands-on missions. Shops, gardens, bridges and water systems each present a practical problem. Children sort, count, measure, group or trade objects; the world responds to what they do.

Progression

Start with one street and a few characters, then unlock connected neighbourhoods as different skills are practised. A repaired bridge becomes usable; a garden grows; a shop becomes stocked. Replay remains available. Unlocks and rewards are proposals, not established rules.

Player workflow

Explore → meet a character with a problem → manipulate objects → see a consequence or helpful hint → complete the mission → watch the village improve.

Visual direction

A warm, layered village with expressive original residents, readable objects and visible before/after changes. Keep essential items distinct from decorative scenery.

Why it could work

Strong connection between learning and useful action; flexible setting for many topics without presenting unrelated quiz menus.

Main risk and first boundary

Scope can grow into too many systems. Start with one shop mission and one clear interaction, not a complete economy, town simulator or five-year curriculum.

Lost Island Expedition

Game function

Explore an original island and repair paths or habitats. Fit shape-based bridges, lay measured tracks, interpret clues and combine information to reach discoveries.

Progression

Move from beach to forest, caves and observatory as skills combine. New areas should introduce new learning patterns, not merely repeat the same question with larger numbers.

Player workflow

Explore → encounter an obstacle → inspect clues → build or test a solution → traverse the repaired route → discover the next place.

Visual direction

Distinct biomes, readable landmarks, satisfying route repair and a friendly companion. The environment should reward curiosity without hiding necessary learning behind difficult movement.

Why it could work

A strong sense of discovery and visible purpose for solving a problem.

Main risk and first boundary

Exploration, camera and traversal add development cost. Movement skill must not become the gate that prevents a child from doing the learning task.

Little Inventors Workshop

Game function

Build scales, ramps, marble tracks, simple water routes or carts. Children choose and arrange parts, test what happens, then revise their design.

Progression

Begin with a few guided parts and one obvious goal, then introduce constraints and several valid solutions. Later challenges can combine measurement, balance and planning.

Player workflow

Receive a challenge → inspect available parts → construct → test → observe → revise → save or celebrate the successful design.

Visual direction

An inviting tactile workshop with chunky parts, clear connection points, animated test runs and visible cause-and-effect. Show measurements and forces only at the appropriate learner level.

Why it could work

Very interactive; encourages experimentation instead of guessing an answer.

Main risk and first boundary

Physics and feedback must be accurate enough not to teach misconceptions. Open construction also needs strong constraints, clear controls and many edge-case tests.

Story Explorers

Game function

Enter an illustrated story, meet characters, examine objects, listen or read, sequence events and make evidence-based choices that affect the scene. Local stories could be considered with appropriate content review.

Progression

Progress from picture-led actions and simple sequences to longer clues, inference and branching consequences. Keep each chapter short enough to understand and replay.

Player workflow

Enter the scene → meet a character → explore, listen or read → choose or construct a response → see the story react → resolve the chapter → continue.

Visual direction

Expressive original characters, focused scene composition, gentle animation and meaningful object interactions rather than long walls of text.

Why it could work

A natural fit for language comprehension and emotional connection.

Main risk and first boundary

Writing, voice production, translation and branching content can expand quickly. Narrative choices need clear educational intent, not arbitrary rewards.

PAWS & PATHS

Game function

The child controls an original kitten exploring a neighbourhood and helping animal friends. Learning happens by manipulating the world, not by answering quiz panels beside a decorative cat.

First possible mission

Rain has damaged the path to a treehouse. Inspect the gap, collect suitable bridge pieces, place them, test the crossing and guide the other kittens to the treehouse. A plank that is too short leaves a visible gap: the child sees why it fails and tries again, without losing lives. Assisted movement keeps platforming skill from blocking the learning activity.

Progression

A proposed route grows from home garden to neighbourhood, market, riverside and treehouse. Helping friends opens routes and earns decorations. Exact rewards, gates and order are not approved. Each area should add a meaningful problem, not merely reskin the same question.

Player workflow

Explore → discover a friend’s need → inspect the situation → collect and manipulate objects → test → see the world respond → complete the mission → reveal a new route.

Possible class-level variants

Early variants could use counting and shape matching, followed by ordering or adding quantities. Later variants could involve measuring and grouping, fractional lengths, material budgets, then multiple length/cost constraints. These are discussion examples across Classes 1–5, not a verified curriculum sequence. Choose one learner level and one objective for the first mission.

Visual direction

Layered 2D scenery, an expressive original cat, small environmental animations and a readable bridge puzzle. The cat design must be our own, not a copy of the Tarang mascot. Show the gap, connection points and result clearly beneath the decorative art.

Strength and risk

The companion creates emotional connection while a visible world problem gives learning a purpose. The risk is drifting into a platformer or physics toy where dexterity and simulation quirks obscure the lesson. Use assisted movement, predictable interactions and carefully checked constraints.

Smallest useful prototype

One original cat, one garden scene, one bridge interaction and one clear ending. No full neighbourhood, five-year curriculum or extra game systems yet. This is a new concept raised in discussion, not an implementation decision.

Class-level progression: a proposal to validate

Classes 1–5 are the confirmed audience, but the actual curriculum and learner prerequisites are not yet established. The bands below are discussion examples, not a certified syllabus mapping.

Provisional bandPossible learning actionsSupport
Classes 1–2Count, sort, recognize shapes; follow simple picture/word instructionsConcrete objects, short prompts, worked examples and generous hints
Class 3Grouping, multiplication, money and measurement tasksMore than one step; visual grouping and clear units
Classes 4–5Fractions, area, budgets and multi-step decisionsExplain constraints, test solutions and reflect on the result

Do not use class alone as a reliable measure of ability. Confirm content with an educator, allow replay and suitable support, and avoid labelling children from an unvalidated score.

Possible first vertical slice

Recommendation for discussion: Living Village with island-like exploration, starting with one shop mission. A younger learner could assemble a requested quantity of objects; a later variant could group quantities or work within a simple budget. These are different learning tasks sharing one setting, not one task made harder only by a timer.

Choose one exact class/skill variant first. Define the request, available objects, interactions, valid solutions, hints, failure recovery and visible world change. The shop is a proposal, not an approved replacement for the earlier starter.

Screen and interaction flow

Proposed common structure: welcome → small explorable hub → mission introduction → optional practice → hands-on task → consequence/hint → completion/world change → replay or another mission. Keep help and sound controls reachable, with a predictable back/exit action. Do not add a map, profile, leaderboard or unlock system merely because it appears in the reference screenshots.

Portrait reference screens do not select the final orientation. Prototype the chosen interaction on the intended phone/tablet/desktop before finalizing layout or camera behaviour.

Art, audio and language plan

  • Create original layered environments, mascots and interaction assets. The reference screenshots are not a licensed production asset library.
  • Use animation to explain consequences, with clear static feedback and reduced-motion alternatives where appropriate.
  • Select the first language before voice production. Marathi-first with English support was an earlier suggestion, not an approved requirement; Hindi is likewise uncommitted.
  • Extract all UI, hints and content into localization data. Verify font glyphs, Devanagari shaping where needed, wrapping, pronunciation and native proofreading.
  • Provide written/pictured equivalents for audio and replay/mute controls. Validate colour contrast, focus visibility, touch targets and reading load.

Technology and data boundaries

The earlier exploratory prototype uses Unity 2D, but the final concept’s technical fit should be checked before treating that as a fixed production decision. Offline Android first with desktop development was a proposal; browser delivery requires its own testing. No platform build is currently verified.

Prefer a minimal, offline-first prototype. Accounts, child profiles, ads, purchases, chat, analytics, public leaderboards and cloud sync are not automatically in scope. Any pupil tracking or teacher dashboard requires separate data and privacy design. Local activity counters are not mastery evidence.

Development workflow after idea selection

  1. Choose the experience: concept, learner/class, subject and first mission.
  2. Specify the mission loop: actions, progression, hints, outcomes and acceptance criteria.
  3. Make a simple interaction prototype: test manipulation and feedback before producing a large art set.
  4. Polish one vertical slice: original visuals, audio if approved, onboarding, exit/retry and completion.
  5. Test on the primary device: touch, layout, performance, interruption, save and accessibility.
  6. Review content and usability: educator review and appropriately supervised learner feedback, without claiming proven outcomes from a small test.
  7. Expand only what works: add skill variants and connected missions after evidence, not all Classes 1–5 content at once.

Quality gates

Record source checks, native compile, play tests and device builds separately. Verify accurate answers or physical relationships, units, narration, repeated taps, back/replay, interruption recovery, persistence, clean reset, responsive controls and helpful error states. Test every allowed solution where a task is open-ended. A beautiful screenshot is not proof of correct gameplay.

Questions for the next discussion

  1. Which experience feels most exciting: village, island, inventions, stories or the cat-focused PAWS & PATHS?
  2. What should the child physically do in the first mission?
  3. Which class, learning objective and syllabus should that mission serve?
  4. What should visibly change after success, and what happens after a mistake?
  5. Which language, primary device and orientation do we design for first?
  6. What evidence will make the first mission good enough to expand?
